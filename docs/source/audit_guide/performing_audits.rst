
==================================
Performing a Bioinformatics Audit
==================================
To perform an audit for a bioinformatics process, you will already need a schedule and an approved checklist. You will also ideally have staff who are trained and competent to perform an audit as objectively as possible. 

Competency for this can look different depending on your operational context. Those with laboratory quality experience may know how to run an audit but not have the bioinformatics knowledge to assess what “correct” looks like at each stage of the bioinformatics scope. Those with bioinformatics expertise may understand the process intimately but have no formal audit training and sit too close to the work to be objective. Some staff hold both skill sets - if so, the training gap narrows, but it may remain tricky to be impartial, since the person best placed to audit the process may also be the personal who built it. 

Whoever designs, performs, and reports on the audit should be independent of the activity being audited, so that findings are impartial and credible. Where this isn't possible, for example, for those with sole or close responsibility for a bioinformatics process, consider pairing an experienced auditor with someone who can explain what the evidence means, or arrange a cross-audit with an external bioinformatician. 

This process applies regardless of your operational context, as bioinformatics can range from a single laptop to a distributed HPC/cloud environment. You can perform an audit on paper, a local spreadsheet, or an electronic quality management system, so long as you can gather adequate evidence. In any case, consider creating a folder, physical or digital, to store the checklist and evidence.

Aim to minimise disruption to service by giving notice to those responsible for the bioinformatics scope and any other relevant parties, e.g. quality management teams, or downstream users of results. The audit schedule should serve as an anchor for this, so disruption is planned for, rather than risk significantly disrupting service. 

The following steps below outline how you may go about performing an audit - that is, answering the questions of the checklist and gathering evidence to answer each question.

* Select a sample at random from a run within the audit schedule. Consider whether one sample sufficiently represents the risk across a run for your context and select more if not. For example, you could select a few samples from the last 10 runs processed in an audit schedule. This will depend on whether your process is low throughput or high throughput - consider this when deciding how to select a representative sample. 
* Go through each checklist question. This should feel like following the bioinformatics sample journey end-to-end.
* Gather evidence for each question. Evidence can be a screenshot, an email, a spreadsheet, a URL, a log file, an SOP or policy document. Whichever form of evidence is selected, ensure whoever reviews the audit later can access it to verify it.
* Provide reasoning alongside the evidence. A simple "Yes/No" of the observation plus a short explanation is sufficient. 
* For each question, decide whether the evidence and reasoning show the process to be Compliant, Non-Compliant, or Not Applicable (N/A). For example, if a checklist question asked whether the software used for analysis is recorded and consistent for a given process, and there is no record of this, and the software or its version unexpectedly differs each time the process is run, this would be marked Non-Compliant
* Ensure the audit runs within a reasonable timeframe, balanced against any planned disruption to service, including staff time.
